01 / Speed of Change

How Fast Is
Everything Moving?

You can't position yourself correctly without understanding the rate of change. The 2022–2026 window was a structural break. But the 2026–2027 candidate problem is different: models are becoming interchangeable faster, while inference economics, evaluation, GPU utilization, distributed systems, security and domain execution are becoming more important.

2022
Slow Burn

ML is a specialist field. GPT-3 is impressive but niche. Junior devs hired aggressively. AI is a "future" conversation. AI adoption is no longer a niche trend; the 2026 Stanford AI Index reports organizational AI adoption at 88%.

2023
Ignition

ChatGPT triggers mass adoption. Companies begin AI experiments and pilot programs. Panic about "AI replacing engineers" starts. Hiring still strong for juniors.

2024–25
Structural Break

Software-developer employment for ages 22–25 has fallen nearly 20% from 2024, while the 2026 PwC AI Jobs Barometer reports a 62% average wage premium for workers with AI skills.

2026+
Acceleration

AI moves from model access to systems economics. Teams care about inference throughput, latency, GPU utilization, reliability, evaluation, security, cost per task and operational ownership. Agent demos are cheap; dependable AI systems are not.

03 / Candidate Calibration

The CRACKED
Standard — Measurable or It Doesn't Count.

This is not a list of admirable traits. It is a qualification battery. The candidate must repeatedly produce correct work under time pressure, without an assistant, with incomplete information, while being deliberately attacked by tests and questioned by another engineer. “I understand it” has zero evidentiary value until the candidate can prove it.

THE CRACKED CONTRACT: For every core capability, the candidate must pass five tests: DERIVE → BUILD → MEASURE → BREAK → DEFEND. Failing any one of the five means the capability is not fully owned.

03.1 — The Five Tests of Ownership

TestWhat the candidate must doWhat counts as passingAutomatic failure
DERIVE Explain the mechanism from first principles and state its assumptions, complexity and failure modes. Can answer “why?” repeatedly without falling back to framework behavior. Appeals to documentation, implementation details or authority instead of reasoning.
BUILD Implement the critical mechanism under a constrained environment. Correct implementation, tests included, within the assigned time. Copies a tutorial, cannot debug their own code, or cannot explain a critical generated component.
MEASURE Define a baseline, metric, experimental protocol and evidence threshold before optimizing. Benchmark is reproducible and conclusions match the evidence. Cherry-picked run, missing baseline, moving goalposts or unsupported “better” claim.
BREAK Design inputs and operating conditions intended to make the system fail. Failure taxonomy exists; failures are reproducible; mitigations are regression-tested. Only tests the happy path.
DEFEND Defend architecture, metrics, implementation and trade-offs under hostile questioning. Can explain rejected alternatives and change position when evidence disproves an assumption. Confuses confidence with evidence or cannot explain why a design exists.

03.2 — Hard Competency Specifications

01

Mathematical + Algorithmic Ownership

The candidate must be able to do this cold
  • Derive gradients for linear/logistic regression, softmax cross-entropy and a two-layer network.
  • Derive the computational and memory complexity of self-attention.
  • Explain why normalization, residual connections and initialization affect optimization.
  • Reason about bias/variance, leakage, calibration, confidence intervals and uncertainty.
  • Analyze algorithmic complexity and identify the dominant term.
  • Explain floating-point error, overflow/underflow and why numerical instability occurs.
Mandatory builds
  • autograd scalar engine;
  • linear/logistic regression from scratch;
  • two-layer neural network with manual backprop;
  • selected data structures without library shortcuts.
Gate battery

3-hour closed-book. One derivation problem, one unfamiliar algorithm, one implementation, one debugging problem and one oral defense.

CRACKED threshold

At least 80% of the written battery, all critical derivations logically correct, and no critical implementation bug left unexplained.

Automatic fail

Cannot derive the mechanism they claim to understand.

02

ML/DL Engineering Ownership

The candidate must be able to
  • Choose a baseline before selecting a sophisticated architecture.
  • Diagnose whether failure originates in data, objective, optimization, architecture or evaluation.
  • Design ablations that isolate causal contributions.
  • Explain training instability from observed curves and system telemetry.
  • Distinguish optimization failure from generalization failure.
  • Build reproducible training with configs, seeds, checkpoints and experiment records.
Mandatory builds
  • from-scratch training loop;
  • controlled architecture ablation;
  • dataset leakage test;
  • reproducibility package.
Gate battery

Given a deliberately broken training run, diagnose the root cause before modifying code. Then design the minimum experiment needed to falsify the diagnosis.

CRACKED threshold

Diagnosis must be evidence-backed; proposed experiment must isolate the suspected cause; final conclusion must survive counter-evidence.

Automatic fail

“Try a larger model / more epochs / different optimizer” without a causal hypothesis.

03

Data Engineering Ownership

The candidate must be able to
  • Define a dataset contract and reject invalid records.
  • Detect leakage, duplication, schema drift and distribution shift.
  • Explain partitioning, shuffles, joins, indexes and query plans.
  • Design replayable pipelines with lineage and versioned artifacts.
  • Reason about batch vs streaming semantics and failure recovery.
Mandatory build

Pipeline: ingestion → validation → transformation → storage → training/evaluation dataset → lineage → replay.

Break battery

Inject malformed schema, duplicated records, missing partitions, late events and distribution shift. The pipeline must detect or safely handle each class.

CRACKED threshold

No silent corruption. Every rejected class has a reason, metric and recovery path.

Automatic fail

Data quality is checked only by manually opening a CSV or notebook.

04

LLM / RAG Ownership

The candidate must be able to
  • Explain attention, tokenization, context limitations and inference behavior.
  • Separate retrieval failure from generation failure.
  • Measure retrieval recall/precision-like task metrics before judging generation.
  • Diagnose chunking, query, reranking, context-packing and citation failures.
  • Control context budgets and reason about latency/cost consequences.
Mandatory build

RAG system with multiple retrieval baselines, reranking, evaluation set, citation checking, failure taxonomy and regression suite.

Gate battery

Given 25–50 deliberately difficult queries, classify every failure as retrieval, ranking, context, generation, grounding or evaluation failure.

CRACKED threshold

Every failure receives an evidence-backed category; proposed fix targets the actual layer rather than blindly changing the model.

Automatic fail

“Use a better LLM” is the default answer to retrieval failures.

05

Agent Systems Ownership

The candidate must be able to
  • Represent agent state explicitly rather than hiding it in prompts.
  • Design typed tools, authorization boundaries and deterministic control paths.
  • Handle retries, timeouts, partial failure, checkpointing and human approval.
  • Evaluate both final outcomes and intermediate trajectories.
  • Reproduce and debug a failed run from an event trace.
Mandatory build

Agent runtime with typed tools, persistent state, event log, replay, retry policy, approval gate, failure recovery and automated evaluation.

Break battery
  • tool returns malformed data;
  • tool becomes unavailable;
  • context becomes inconsistent;
  • instruction conflicts with policy;
  • agent enters a loop;
  • tool attempts an unauthorized action.
CRACKED threshold

Every failure terminates safely, leaves an auditable trace and enters the regression suite.

Automatic fail

Agent succeeds in a demo but has no deterministic failure/recovery model.

06

Inference + GPU Ownership

The candidate must be able to
  • Explain prefill vs decode and identify compute- vs memory-bound regimes.
  • Reason about KV-cache memory and batching.
  • Read a profiler trace and identify the dominant bottleneck.
  • Explain occupancy, register pressure, memory bandwidth and launch overhead.
  • Understand quantization and speculative decoding as measured trade-offs.
  • Connect high-level serving behavior to accelerator-level execution.
Mandatory builds
  • CUDA vector/reduction/GEMM primitive;
  • Triton kernel;
  • inference benchmark harness;
  • quantization comparison;
  • profiler-based optimization.
Gate battery

Given a latency/throughput target, produce a baseline, profile it, identify the bottleneck, implement one optimization and prove the improvement.

CRACKED threshold

No optimization is accepted without profiler evidence and reproducible benchmark conditions.

Automatic fail

Only knows serving flags; cannot explain the hardware/software bottleneck.

07

Distributed Systems Ownership

The candidate must be able to
  • Explain data, tensor, pipeline, expert and context parallelism.
  • Reason about communication/computation overlap and scaling efficiency.
  • Understand collective communication and topology effects.
  • Handle worker failure, checkpoint/restart and stragglers.
  • Calculate why scaling stops being linear.
Mandatory build

Multi-GPU workload with scaling curve, communication measurements and injected worker failure.

Gate battery

Explain a measured scaling curve from single GPU through multiple GPUs and identify where communication becomes dominant.

CRACKED threshold

Can predict the next scaling bottleneck before testing it.

Automatic fail

“Distributed training means add more GPUs” is the mental model.

08

Production / SRE Ownership

The candidate must be able to
  • Define SLIs/SLOs for latency, availability, correctness and AI quality.
  • Trace a request across model, retrieval, tools and infrastructure.
  • Deploy with canary, rollback and health checks.
  • Reason about capacity, queueing and resource limits.
  • Run an incident, contain damage and write a useful postmortem.
Mandatory build

Kubernetes AI service with telemetry, autoscaling, canary, rollback, secrets/IAM and incident runbook.

Break battery

Traffic spike + failed pod + bad model release + dependency timeout. Candidate must restore service without ad-hoc SSH heroics.

CRACKED threshold

Failure is detected, bounded, recovered and converted into a permanent regression/control.

Automatic fail

No rollback path or no visibility into latency/cost/quality.

09

AI Security Ownership

The candidate must be able to
  • Threat-model model, data, tools, identity, network and supply chain.
  • Demonstrate prompt injection and tool-abuse paths.
  • Design least-privilege tool permissions and trust boundaries.
  • Detect data exfiltration and unsafe tool execution.
  • Turn successful attacks into regression tests.
Mandatory build

Attack/defense harness with threat model, attack corpus, mitigations, telemetry and regression suite.

CRACKED threshold

At least three distinct attack classes are reproduced, mitigated and regression-tested.

Automatic fail

Security is a final checklist instead of an architectural property.

10

Research Ownership

The candidate must be able to
  • Identify the exact claim a paper makes.
  • Reproduce the baseline with controlled environment and seeds.
  • Design ablations that isolate mechanisms.
  • Quantify uncertainty and effect size.
  • Identify benchmark leakage, saturation or evaluator weakness.
  • Produce a negative result without manipulating the narrative.
Mandatory build

One full reproduction + ≥3 meaningful ablations + one extension or negative result + public reproducibility package.

CRACKED threshold

A technically stronger reviewer must be able to attack the methodology and you must be able to answer with evidence.

Automatic fail

Paper summary presented as research.

11

Technical Judgment

The candidate must be able to
  • Choose the simplest architecture that satisfies the constraints.
  • State what they are optimizing: quality, latency, cost, reliability, safety or scientific value.
  • Explain build-vs-buy and model-selection decisions.
  • Quantify major cost drivers.
  • State when the system should not be used.
Mandatory artifact

Architecture decision record containing requirements, alternatives, rejected options, quantitative trade-offs, risks and reversal conditions.

CRACKED threshold

Can argue both for and against their own design.

Automatic fail

Technology choice is justified by popularity rather than constraints.

12

Communication Under Fire

The candidate must be able to
  • Explain a complex system at implementation, architecture and executive levels.
  • Answer hostile “why not X?” questions.
  • Distinguish measured fact, assumption, inference and speculation.
  • Admit uncertainty without losing technical control.
  • Change an argument when evidence disproves it.
Mandatory test

10-minute architecture defense followed by 15 minutes of adversarial questioning.

CRACKED threshold

No hand-waving. Every important claim maps to code, measurement, equation, experiment or explicit assumption.

Automatic fail

Bluffing when the evidence is absent.

03.5 — Automatic Disqualification Behaviors

  • Benchmark theater: selecting the metric or run that makes the result look best.
  • Framework camouflage: using a library abstraction to conceal lack of mechanism-level understanding.
  • AI laundering: presenting generated work as personally reasoned without review or reconstruction.
  • Demo fraud: showing a happy-path demo while hiding known failure modes.
  • Research cosplay: summarizing papers without reproducing or challenging their claims.
  • Stack collecting: listing technologies without an artifact demonstrating independent use.
  • Metric shopping: changing the evaluation criterion after seeing the result.
  • Complexity worship: choosing a sophisticated architecture when a simpler one satisfies the constraints.
  • Unowned code: inability to explain or debug a critical component in the portfolio.
  • False certainty: stating an inference or assumption as though it were measured fact.

08 / Daily Operating System

How to Structure
Every Single Day

The gap between an average ML engineer and an untouchable one isn't talent — it's daily structure. You need a system that builds depth and produces artifacts simultaneously. Here is that system.

The single biggest mistake engineers make: using AI to skip the hard thinking. Every time you let a model do your architecture reasoning, you atrophy the muscle that makes you worth $300K.

05:30–07:00
🔇
Deep Work · No AI tools · No phone Architecture Thinking & Hard Problems

The most cognitively demanding work goes here — design decisions, algorithm choices, system architecture. This is the session that makes you irreplaceable. Use AI tools only after you've committed your own reasoning to paper first.

07:00–07:45
📄
Deliberate Reading · One Paper or Technical Deep-Dive Stay at the Frontier

One paper from arXiv, one in-depth engineering blog post, or one chapter of a technical book — read with a pen. Write 3 sentences of your own reaction: what's the key insight, what assumption is being made, what would you challenge?

07:45–10:00
⚙️
Build Block · AI tools permitted for execution only Production Engineering — Code, Systems, Deployment

Implement what you designed in Block 1. MLOps pipelines, model training, API development, infrastructure. AI tools handle boilerplate. You handle architecture and debugging logic. Document every non-trivial decision.

10:00–11:30
🧪
Experimentation · Evaluation · Monitoring Own the Production Layer

Run experiments properly — not just "does it work" but "why does it work." Set up monitoring for drift. Write evaluation scripts. Learn what your systems look like when they fail. This is where most engineers are lazy, and where you become indispensable.

13:00–14:30
🌐
Cross-Domain Study · Rotate Weekly Your Domain Depth Block

Week 1: Domain deep-dive (healthcare, fintech, robotics — whichever you've chosen). Week 2: Systems thinking / distributed systems. Week 3: AI safety / alignment / governance. Week 4: Math fundamentals (probability theory, information theory).

14:30–15:30
✍️
Writing Block · 400+ words · Publish or archive Build the Public Record

One technical writeup per week. Architecture decision records, failure post-mortems, implementation notes, design essays. Even private writing forces precision of thought. Public writing builds reputation. Both compound permanently.

15:30–17:00
🏗️
Open Source / Personal Projects / Systems Work Build Artifacts That Outlast You

Contribute to a real open-source ML project or build your own framework/tool. Not toy projects — systems with real users or real utility. Every production system you build encodes your architectural judgment. These are permanent resume items.

21:00–21:30
🪞
Reflection · 3 questions · 10 minutes Daily Compounding Protocol

Three questions: (1) What did I understand today that I didn't yesterday? (2) What did I let AI think for me that I should have thought myself? (3) What is the hardest question in my current work that I haven't answered yet? Write tomorrow's deep work problem tonight.

04B / Detailed Curriculum

What “Learn It”
Actually Means.

Every module is learned through six layers: concept → implementation → systems behavior → measurement → adversarial testing → proof. Reading material is preparation, never completion.

CONCEPTMechanism, equations, assumptions, trade-offs.
IMPLEMENTBuild the critical core with minimal scaffolding.
SYSTEMScale, failure, resource and operational behavior.
MEASUREBaseline, metrics, controls and uncertainty.
BREAKAdversarial inputs, overload, attacks and edge cases.

L0 · Foundations
01 — Mathematics, CS and Programming
  • Math: linear algebra, SVD/eigensystems, probability/Bayes, estimation, hypothesis tests, calculus, gradients/Hessians, convexity, SGD/Adam, numerical stability and floating-point behavior.
  • CS: complexity, graphs, trees, hashing, DP, processes/threads, memory, filesystems, sockets, transactions, indexes, isolation, concurrency, locks, queues and backpressure.
  • Programming: Python internals, testing, packaging, profiling, debugging; C++ RAII, templates, STL, move semantics, memory and concurrency; build systems and sanitizers.
  • Build: data-structure library + scalar autograd + concurrent producer/consumer system.
  • Gate: 3-hour closed-book implementation + derivation exam + oral defense.
L1 · ML/DL
02 — Machine Learning and Deep Learning
  • Classical: regression, logistic models, SVM, trees, boosting, clustering and dimensionality reduction.
  • Deep: MLPs, CNNs, normalization, residuals, attention, transformers, ViTs, diffusion/generative modeling and multimodality.
  • Training: losses/gradients, initialization, optimizers, schedules, mixed precision, checkpointing, accumulation and distributed basics.
  • Science: leakage, baselines, seeds, confidence intervals, effect sizes, calibration, uncertainty and error decomposition.
  • Build: classical algorithms + MLP/CNN/attention from scratch + optimization study.
L2 · Data
03 — Data Engineering for AI
  • Storage: object storage, partitions, formats, schema evolution and data contracts.
  • Quality: completeness, validity, uniqueness, freshness, distribution checks and anomaly detection.
  • Lineage: dataset/version tracking, provenance, reproducibility and replay.
  • SQL: joins, windows, indexes, query plans, transactions and isolation.
  • Streaming: Kafka partitions, ordering, consumer groups, retries and delivery semantics.
  • Distributed: Spark execution, shuffles, partitioning and skew; retrieval corpus construction and evaluation.
  • Build: pipeline with quality gates, lineage, replay and corruption tests.
L3 · LLM/Agents
04 — LLM and Agent Engineering
  • Model: attention, positional methods, normalization, residuals, MoE concepts, tokenization and context behavior.
  • RAG: retrieval quality, query transformation, reranking, context packing, citation fidelity and retrieval evaluation.
  • Agents: state machines, tools, memory, checkpoints, retries, long-running jobs and human approval.
  • Protocols: MCP-style capability discovery, schemas, authorization and trust boundaries.
  • Evaluation: outcome, trajectory, tool-call correctness, recovery, long-horizon degradation and cost.
  • Build: agent runtime with typed tools, state log, replay, eval harness and failure recovery.
L4 · Inference
05 — LLM Inference Engineering
  • Execution: prefill/decode, compute vs memory bound behavior, scheduling and admission control.
  • Memory: KV cache, paged attention, prefix caching, cache pressure/eviction and KV offload.
  • Serving: continuous batching, chunked prefill, structured outputs, LoRA, routing and failure handling.
  • Optimization: FP8/INT8/INT4, speculative decoding, CUDA graphs, fused kernels and quantization trade-offs.
  • Distributed: tensor/pipeline/data/expert/context parallelism; disaggregated prefill/decode and KV transfer.
  • Metrics: TTFT, inter-token latency, queue time, throughput, utilization, memory and cost/token.
  • Build: benchmark multiple serving configurations and explain every delta.
L4 · GPU
06 — CUDA, Triton and Kernel Engineering
  • Architecture: warps, blocks, scheduling, synchronization, memory hierarchy and occupancy.
  • Memory: coalescing, shared memory, registers, caches and bandwidth ceilings.
  • CUDA: streams, events, async copies, CUDA graphs, mixed precision and tensor-core concepts.
  • Triton: tiling, masking, reductions, autotuning and generated-kernel reasoning.
  • Low level: PTX/SASS literacy sufficient to connect source choices to generated instructions.
  • Profiling: Nsight methodology and roofline/bottleneck reasoning.
  • Build: vector op → reduction → GEMM → softmax → attention primitive.
L5 · Distributed AI
07 — Multi-GPU, Networking and Scheduling
  • Parallelism: DDP, FSDP/sharding, tensor/pipeline/expert/context parallelism.
  • Communication: collective operations, NCCL concepts, topology and overlap.
  • Networking: RDMA concepts, latency/bandwidth and interconnect topology.
  • Reliability: checkpointing, restart, stragglers, worker failure and fault tolerance.
  • Scheduling: queues, quotas, GPU fragmentation, utilization and workload priority; DRA/Kueue concepts.
  • Build: multi-GPU scaling experiment with injected failure.
L5–L6 · Platform
08 — Kubernetes and AI Platform Engineering
  • Core: pods, deployments, services, Gateway API, scheduling, volumes, RBAC and namespaces.
  • Resources: requests/limits, autoscaling, node pools, taints/tolerations and topology.
  • AI infrastructure: DRA, accelerator claims, device-aware scheduling and workload queues.
  • AI Gateway: model routing, policy, authentication, rate limiting, retries and inference-aware scheduling.
  • Operations: Helm, GitOps, secrets, workload identity, canaries, rollback and disaster recovery.
  • Build: Kubernetes AI platform with inference, telemetry, canary/rollback and accelerator scheduling.
L6–L7 · Reliability/Security
09 — AI SRE, Security and Governance
  • SRE: SLIs/SLOs for latency, availability, correctness, quality and safety; incidents and postmortems.
  • Observability: model/tool/retrieval/cache/token/GPU traces correlated to user outcomes.
  • Security: prompt injection, exfiltration, tool abuse, privilege escalation, supply chain, identity and network boundaries.
  • Governance: model/data provenance, risk registers and standards literacy.
  • Build: attack/defense harness with telemetry and regression suite.
L8 · Research
10 — Research Engineering
  • Paper analysis: claim, mechanism, assumptions, evidence, baseline and limitations.
  • Reproduction: exact environment, seeds, preprocessing, configs and evaluation.
  • Ablation: controls, interactions, effect sizes, uncertainty and multiple-comparison discipline.
  • Benchmark science: leakage, saturation, contamination, invalid questions and evaluator bias.
  • Software: configs, tests, checkpointing and reproducible experiment registry.
  • Build: reproduction + ≥3 ablations + extension/negative result + public report.
L9 · Domain/Strategy
11 — Domain, Economics and Technical Judgment
  • Domain: workflows, stakeholders, constraints, failure costs, data provenance and regulation.
  • Economics: cost/request, cost/token, GPU utilization, capacity, value generated and build-vs-buy.
  • Product: quality, task completion, trust and business outcomes.
  • Strategy: architecture choices, vendor lock-in, deprecation, capacity planning and roadmap prioritization.
  • Build: domain system + technical strategy memo + unit economics + risk register.

2026 frontier requirement

Inference is a systems discipline. Current vLLM capabilities include prefix caching, chunked prefill, FP8 KV cache, speculative decoding, multiple parallelism modes and disaggregated prefill/decode. Kubernetes has also formalized AI Gateway work around AI-aware networking and inference infrastructure. These are mechanisms to understand and benchmark, not checkbox technologies.

Technical Communication

11 / Iron Rules

Non-Negotiable
Operating Principles

01

Own Every Line of Logic You Ship

If you cannot explain every architectural decision, every model choice, every trade-off in the systems you build — you are a prompt engineer, not an ML engineer. AI-generated code you haven't verified and understood is a liability that will surface in production and in interviews.

02

Production Over Notebooks — Always

A model that works in a Kaggle notebook impresses no one in 2026. Every project should have: a serving layer, monitoring, versioning, and documentation of failure modes. The gap between "works on my machine" and "works for 10,000 users at 3am" is where your value lives.

03

Learn the Failure Modes Before You Ship

The most valuable ML engineers in production environments are the ones who can predict how a system will fail before it does. Study distribution shift, data pipeline failures, feedback loops, model degradation. Know your system's threat model, not just its happy path.

04

Depth in One Domain Over Breadth in All

In a world where AI gives everyone surface-level competence in everything, depth is the only defensible position. Pick one domain — healthcare, fintech, robotics, any vertical — and develop genuine subject matter expertise alongside your ML skills. This intersection is where the salary premium lives.

05

Stay Two Cycles Ahead of the Curriculum

If you're learning what Coursera is teaching, you're 18 months behind the market. Read papers before they become blog posts. Implement techniques before they become libraries. The engineer who understood LoRA before it was in every tutorial had a year-long salary advantage.

06

Every Project Produces a Public Artifact

A GitHub repo with a strong README and real engineering decisions documented. A technical blog post explaining what you built and why. A postmortem of something that failed. These are permanent, compound assets that differentiate you in a market flooded with AI-polished resumes and identical portfolios.

07

Use AI to Accelerate Execution, Never to Replace Thinking

AI tools are the most powerful productivity multiplier in engineering history — for engineers who already have strong judgment. Use them aggressively for boilerplate, debugging, documentation, and exploration. The moment you use them to outsource your architecture decisions, you start becoming replaceable.

08

Hire-Ability is a Skill You Actively Maintain

The hiring bar in 2026 has moved upstream — companies test for thinking, not syntax. Aptitude assessments surged 54x since 2024. Practice system design. Practice explaining complex trade-offs clearly. Practice defending your architectural decisions to an adversarial interviewer. These are perishable skills if you don't exercise them.
